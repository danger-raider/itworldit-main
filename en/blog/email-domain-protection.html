SPF, DKIM and DMARC: why unprotected domain email works against your business

A plain-language explanation of how basic DNS records help reduce email spoofing and improve trust in your domain.

Businesses often treat email as something obvious: the domain is connected, messages are being sent, so everything must be fine. But for domain email, “it works” and “it is protected” are not the same thing.

If SPF, DKIM and DMARC are not configured or are configured chaotically, it becomes easier for attackers to spoof messages from your domain, and harder for mail services to understand which messages can be trusted.

What SPF does

SPF answers a simple question: which servers are allowed to send email on behalf of your domain. For example, if you use Google Workspace, that mail service should be included in the SPF record.

What DKIM does

DKIM adds a cryptographic signature to the message. A mail system can verify whether the email was actually signed by your domain and whether it was changed in transit.

What DMARC does

DMARC defines the policy: what should happen to messages that fail authentication checks. At the beginning, many businesses use a soft policy to collect information, then gradually strengthen the rules.

Common mistakes

  • multiple SPF records instead of one correct record;
  • DKIM is not enabled in the email service;
  • DMARC is missing or configured in a mode that does not control anything;
  • old services remain in DNS after they are no longer used;
  • no one checks whether messages actually pass authentication.

What proper configuration gives you

SPF/DKIM/DMARC do not guarantee absolute phishing protection, but they significantly improve the basic security of domain email. They are the foundation. Without them, other email security measures look like a strong lock on a door without hinges.

Practical takeaway: if a domain is used for business communication, SPF, DKIM and DMARC should not be “someday”. They should be part of the basic launch.
